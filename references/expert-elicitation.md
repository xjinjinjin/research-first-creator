# Expert-practice elicitation

The goal is to capture expert judgment that is normally omitted from generic tutorials.

## Prefer incident-based evidence

When possible, find material where an expert discusses a real difficult case, a decision, a revision, a failure, or a before/after example. These reveal more than broad advice such as "be concise" or "follow best practices".

Use ideas from Cognitive Task Analysis and Critical Decision Method: reconstruct what happened, identify critical cues, and capture how the expert made the judgment.

## Extraction frame

For every important expert mechanism, extract:

1. **Scenario** — What concrete task or difficult situation was happening?
2. **Cues** — What did the expert notice that a novice might miss?
3. **Judgment rule** — How did those cues change the decision?
4. **Action** — What did the expert do next?
5. **Exception** — When would the rule not apply?
6. **Common error** — What do weaker practitioners usually do instead?
7. **Validation** — How does the expert know the result is good enough?

Convert this chain into operational skill instructions only when it is relevant and sufficiently supported.

## Triangulation

Do not promote one expert's personal preference into a universal rule without checking whether:
- authoritative guidance supports it;
- multiple strong practitioners independently use it;
- it solves a recurring failure mode;
- it fits the user's intended context.

When strong experts disagree, preserve the disagreement as a design choice for Round 2 rather than averaging it away.

## Expert-quality signals

Useful signals include:
- primary responsibility for the work being discussed;
- maintained tools, papers, standards, or widely used systems;
- concrete case evidence;
- visible revision history or postmortems;
- explanations of tradeoffs and exceptions;
- independently verifiable outcomes.

Popularity alone is not expertise.
